The projects that feel alive did not invent clever runtimes. They had a lot of art, or they borrowed someone else's.\n\nCount it honestly. VPet walks, climbs and falls, and it does so on roughly 384 hand-drawn PNG animation sets extended by a Steam Workshop full of volunteer artists. Shimeji has the richest behaviour vocabulary in the whole field and it costs 46 hand-drawn frames per character, which is precisely why thousands of Shimeji sets exist and almost nobody finishes a bigger one. Ark-Pets is the best Spine desktop pet in existence and its author drew nothing; it consumes Hypergryph's professional studio rigs. Mate Engine is a polished Unity companion built entirely on the VRM economy. Live2DViewerEX is a commercial business whose entire product is playing back other people's rigs. Of the twelve shipping companions in that table, exactly two contain art the project authors made themselves, pet-mochi (14 poses) and MiniCPM-Desk-Pet (AI generated APNG loops), and both are the least mobile characters in the list.\n\nThe runtime cleverness, meanwhile, is remarkably cheap and remarkably repeated. Standing on window edges is a rectangle intersection test plus gravity; Ark-Pets, Shimeji and Desktop Virtual Buddy each implemented it independently and none of them treats it as hard. Drag and throw is a velocity integration. Action selection in the 1.1k-star Ark-Pets is a weighted random draw with a timer so it does not re-roll mid-animation. There is no secret sauce being withheld from you.\n\nThe second common trait is a hard separation between a local simulation and the language model. pet-mochi is the clearest statement: a deterministic tick every 3 seconds owns mood, needs, movement and sleep, and the LLM is called only at salience moments behind a 90 second cooldown. Every project that made the model the animation driver produces a character that freezes between tokens. Notice also what every single LLM-plus-character project does at the boundary: petto enumerates the model's motion list at load and lets the model pick a name; OpenPets reduces agent events to one of eight states; MiniCPM maps hook events to animation slots; Desktop Virtual Buddy constrains the model to four verbs. Nobody lets a model pick keyframes. Your \"open vocabulary\" should be an open vocabulary of intents resolved by a compositor against the rig's actual clip list, with an explicit fallback when the model invents a verb you never rigged.\n\nThird, and this is the one that should reshape your plan: in every automated chain in this research, the body is solved and the face is not. UniRig, Make-It-Animatable, AccuRIG and Tripo all produce a skeleton and skinning in seconds and none of them produces a facial rig. Tripo's own documentation says it plainly. AnimatedDrawings has no face system whatsoever. Meta's 2.5D rigs paper does not mention faces. Adobe is the mirror image: it gives you a world-class automatic face and lip-sync for free, and its walk system needs three separate views and fights the face system. Stop looking for one tool that does both. Buy or build the face as a separate, head-and-shoulders-sized job.\n\n## 3. Verdict on the 2.5D Spine plan\n\nShort version: the runtime half of your plan is genuinely low risk and the art half is riskier than you think, but the fix is to delete a requirement rather than to spend more.\n\n**What is safe.** spine-threejs is a first-party Esoteric runtime listed as supported, not experimental, and it drops a `SkeletonMesh` straight into the three.js scene you already have; its only stated gap is two-colour tinting, which is irrelevant to a desktop pet. AIRI, a 48.7k-star project, already ships a Spine renderer alongside its Live2D and VRM ones. A commercial Steam product treats Spine as a first-class companion format. Ark-Pets proves the desktop physics layer. None of that is where you lose.\n\n**What the flagship reference actually requires, which is much less than your plan assumes.** Your plan says \"multi-view layers\". Arknights does not do multi-view. Ark-Models chibis have no back-view skeleton and no back-view skin; turning is `skeleton.setScaleX()` flipped on a `mobility` value of plus or minus one. A studio the size of Hypergryph, shipping hundreds of operators, chose side view plus a horizontal mirror. Girls' Frontline and Azur Lane chibis are the same, and their answer to \"more life\" is more animations on one rig, not more viewpoints. Even Esoteric's own four-direction Chibi Stickers example ships one front image set per skin and admits in writing that the body does not turn and the result is not perfect for every skin.\n\nSo the multi-view layer requirement is not what Arknights-style chibis require. It is what you assumed they require, and it is the single most expensive and most artist-dependent line in your plan. Delete it. If you ever need a \"turns away\" beat, do what the vendor does: swap a back-of-head and back-hair attachment for a few frames. That is two extra drawings, not a second rig.\n\n**Licence and money, verified.** You need Spine Professional at $379 (currently discounted from $449). Essential at $69 is a trap: it cannot save or export projects containing meshes, IK or physics constraints, which is the entire reason to choose Spine over sprite frames. Enterprise at $2,499 plus $379 per user per year only applies above $500k revenue. The Runtimes licence requires an Editor licence at the time you integrate; after that you ship to end users freely and they need no licence, and if your licence lapses you may keep distributing the existing product but may not integrate the runtime into new ones. Rigging labour is cheap on the open market: Fiverr Spine 2D rigging and animation gigs advertise from $15 to $25, several explicitly offering idle, walk, run and jump, and several explicitly using Spine Pro.\n\n**The step your plan quietly assumes and cannot do.** \"AI art to human cleanup into layers\" is the step you cannot perform yourself, because it requires drawing the parts that are hidden behind other parts: the back hair behind the head, the arm behind the torso, the leg behind the other leg. That is now partially automated. See-through (SIGGRAPH 2026, Apache-2.0, code and weights public) decomposes one anime character image into up to 23 inpainted PSD layers with inferred draw order, runs on 8 to 16 GB of VRAM or a HuggingFace Space in two to three minutes, and is aimed squarely at Live2D and Spine prep. The honest caveat from an independent hands-on writeup is that it struggles with heavy chibi-style deformation, frills and semi-transparency, and that layer cleanup, ponytail-style parts outside its 19-part vocabulary, and the actual rigging remain manual.\n\n**Feasibility for a solo owner with no artist.** With the multi-view requirement deleted and See-through doing the decomposition, I would call the side-view Spine route a realistic two to four week part-time project, with roughly $400 to $500 of spend ($379 for Spine Pro, $20 to $100 for one rigging and animation commission from the decomposed PSD, plus a few dollars of GPU time). With the multi-view requirement kept, I would call it not feasible without an artist: you would be commissioning three or four consistent views of a character that does not exist yet, at which point you are paying for character art, and every marketplace pack you might buy instead is not your character. This is confidence \"likely\", based on reading tools and prices rather than having run the pipeline.\n\nOne caution I would not skip. Before spending, put an Arknights Spine chibi (via Ark-Pets, free) and an MMD model (via yoMMD, free) on your own desktop this week and look at them for an hour. Both run today. The sharp question is whether a side-view chibi that looks exactly right beats a 3D character that moves in every direction and looks 80 percent right, and that is a taste question only you can answer, cheaply, before any money moves.\n\n## 4. Three candidate new approaches, ranked\n\n### 1. Side-view Spine chibi, auto-decomposed, rigged once by a paid rigger\n\n**What it is.** Your own plan with the two expensive requirements removed. Generate one front or three-quarter view of your chibi with an image model until you love it. Run See-through to get a 23-layer inpainted PSD. Send that PSD to a $20 Fiverr Spine Pro rigger with a written animation list harvested from VPet's `vup.lps` and Shimeji's behaviour XML: idle breathing, walk left, climb, fall, sit, sleep, raised, plus start/loop/end triples. Render it with spine-threejs inside the Electron and three.js app you already have. Turn with `setScaleX`, never show the back, and fake any turn-away with a back-of-head attachment swap.\n\n**Why it fits you.** It is the only route where likeness is guaranteed by construction, because the art starts as your image rather than as someone's parametric avatar. Everything downstream of the rig is proven: spine-threejs is first-party, AIRI already ships a Spine stage, Ark-Pets proves the desktop physics, and the animation list is copied from a shipping product rather than invented.\n\n**Tool or repo that makes it possible.** `shitagaki-lab/see-through` (Apache-2.0) for the decomposition; Esoteric spine-threejs for the runtime; `isHarryh/Ark-Pets` (GPL-3.0, read for the spec, do not copy code into a closed product) for edge-standing and weighted action selection.\n\n**Biggest risk.** See-through's own weak spot is heavy chibi deformation, which is exactly your art style. If it decomposes your chibi badly, you are back to paying for hand cleanup, and the rigger's price goes up accordingly.\n\n**One-day test.** Generate three candidate chibi images. Push each through the See-through HuggingFace Space. Open the PSDs and count how many of the 23 layers are usable without redrawing. If two of three characters give you clean front hair, back hair, face parts, torso and separated limbs, the route is open. If all three smear, stop and re-rank.\n\n### 2. Generated 3D in the runtime you already own, with the face bought separately\n\n**What it is.** Keep Electron plus three.js plus three-vrm exactly as is. Feed your chibi image to StdGEN, which produces an A-pose mesh semantically decomposed into hair, face, body and clothing, trained by rendering VRM models so its conventions already match your runtime. Rig the body automatically with UniRig or AccuRIG. Fill the motion vocabulary from ActorCore's 4,500 free retargetable clips, and generate the long tail overnight with MDM. Then treat the face as a separate purchase: either a blendshape set authored once on the clean face geometry StdGEN gives you, or a hosted generator such as Neural4D that claims a 52-blendshape VRM out of the box.\n\n**Why it fits you.** It is the shortest path from \"AI art of my chibi\" to something that loads in the app you have today, and it is the only candidate that gives you real 3D turning, real hair and cloth physics, and a proper facial rig at the same time. It also converts your rejected Meshy asset from a dead end into an input, since Hunyuan3D-Part's P3-SAM and X-Part exist specifically to split a welded shell into parts.\n\n**Tool or repo that makes it possible.** `hyz317/StdGEN` (Apache-2.0 on both code and HuggingFace weights) is the keystone, because it fixes the exact two failures that killed Meshy: welded shell and inseparable hair and cloth.\n\n**Biggest risk.** Likeness, which is the same thing that killed VRoid. A generated 3D chibi derived from your art is a different failure mode than a parametric VRoid avatar, but it is still a lossy 3D interpretation of a 2D drawing, and chibi proportions plus large hair are where these generators are weakest. Secondary risk: Make-It-Animatable documents degradation on long hair, so decompose hair out before rigging, and Neural4D's blendshape claim is vendor marketing I could not independently verify.\n\n**One-day test.** Run your existing chibi reference through StdGEN, then through UniRig, then load the result in your current three-vrm scene and play one Mixamo walk cycle. The only question that matters at the end of the day is whether you look at it and feel the same \"that is not my character\" reaction you had with VRoid. If yes, this route is closed permanently and you have saved yourself weeks.\n\n### 3. Port the Meta 2.5D rig into TypeScript, with the face as a separate 2D layer\n\n**What it is.** Build the compositor Meta already designed. Take See-through's layered PSD, build a 2D triangle mesh per layer with an ARAP-style deformer and an auto-generated joint rig, the way AnimatedDrawings does from a single mask, then drive it with 3D skeletal motion retargeted view-dependently, the way the 2.5D Character Rigs paper describes, with layer swapping and reordering as the character turns. The face is not part of that rig at all; it is a separate layered puppet composited on the head bone, driven by simple parameters for blink, brow, mouth shape and visemes, in the Adobe Character Animator sense.\n\n**Why it fits you.** Zero art commissions and zero per-character cost forever. It also removes the arbitration bug Adobe's forums document, because your face performance and your locomotion never touch the same rig. Locomotion clips come free from ActorCore, Mixamo or MDM, since the retargeting consumes 3D skeletal motion. And because you own the compositor, adding a new pose is code, not art.\n\n**Tool or repo that makes it possible.** `facebookresearch/AnimatedDrawings` (MIT, including model weights) for the auto-rig and ARAP algorithm to port, read alongside arXiv 2502.17866 for the view-dependent layer logic. Important: the repo was archived on 3 September 2025, so it is reference code and not a dependency, and its Python and OpenGL renderer cannot live in Electron anyway. The valuable part is the rigging step, not the renderer.\n\n**Biggest risk.** This is by far the most engineering, and it is engineering nobody has published in JavaScript. Meta released a paper for the 2.5D version but no code that I could find. You would be the first to build it, which means the risk is schedule, not feasibility.\n\n**One-day test.** Take a See-through PSD, load the layers into your existing three.js scene as textured quads with per-layer depth, hand-place six to eight joints, and skin the layers with a naive weight falloff. Then play one BVH walk cycle from Mixamo through those joints, in 2D, and see whether the silhouette reads as a walking chibi or as a paper doll. That single test tells you whether the ARAP port is worth a week, and it costs you one day either way.\n\n**Why this ranking.** Option 1 is first because it is the only one where the likeness question is already answered before you spend, and every part of it except See-through is proven by a shipping project. Option 2 is second because it is cheapest to test and reuses your entire existing runtime, but it re-runs the exact experiment that already failed once. Option 3 is last on schedule risk alone; it is the most interesting and the most likely to still be unfinished in three months.\n\nTwo rules I would apply to all three. Put the renderer behind a small RPC seam, the way petto talks to Kage over a local WebSocket with `setModel`, `listMotions`, `triggerMotion`, `setExpression`, `showText`, so the character format stays a runtime choice, as it is in AIRI and in the shipping Steam product. And build the brain first against your existing VRM model using Desktop Virtual Buddy's four verbs, `behavior`, `walkTo`, `jumpToWindow`, `say`, with pet-mochi's local simulation underneath it. That way if art route number five is also rejected, you lose a component and not the project.\n\n## 5.
